Case study · Mobile app · Healthcare workforce

Making a shift impossible to misread.

Per-diem and contract clinicians check their phone between patients. They need three answers fast: what am I working, can I clock in, will these hours count. I designed the worker app so each answer has exactly one place to live.

The 30-second version

Workers could not tell an assigned shift from an open one, a normal clock-in from a callback, or a pending day from a finished one. Every ambiguity became a message to a scheduler or an argument with payroll.

I owned the worker app end to end: the journey from login to a closed timesheet, the state model behind every screen, interaction design for 84 screens, the clickable prototype, and the handoff file with empty, pop-up and success states.

  • Colour is the calendarFive day states, one legend, one card. The tab changes the list, never the calendar.
  • The punch names its exceptionCallback, location and approval are written on the card before the button is pressed.
  • A day says if it is finishedFive timesheet statuses share one layout. Edit is the only correction.

Process

Enumerate the states first. Then draw.

The riskiest thing in a time-and-pay app is a state nobody designed. I listed every state a day can be in before drawing a screen, and treated each unanswered question as a bug.

What I assumed, and how I would test it

I worked from the flow and the platform rules, so I wrote my assumptions as testable claims instead of passing them off as findings.

  • Glanceable beats completeWorkers open the app for seconds. Test: time-to-answer on three tasks.
  • Colour alone is not enoughFive states need a second cue. Test: the colour-vision check in System.
  • Callback is common enoughIt earns its own button. Test: share of punches that are callbacks.

    Three rules the screens quietly enforce.

    Rule 01 · Colour is the calendar

    Five states, readable at a glance, and readable without colour.

    Blue is assigned, red is open, teal is available, violet is less preferred, amber is N/A. Filter a state, then run the week through a colour-vision simulation. If the legend were the only cue, this would break. That is the gap I would close next.

    What I would do differently

    Five colours on one week is the design's biggest bet and its biggest accessibility risk. The text legend helps, but a glyph on each day would make the state readable without colour. I would test both versions before any further visual polish.

    Clinicians work in basements and stairwells. A punch that cannot reach the server should be queued, labelled as such, and reconciled later. The current flow names location problems but does not yet name connectivity ones.

    The four events in the plan above are cheap to add and would turn every future design debate into a data question. I would ship the instrumentation with the first release, not after it.
